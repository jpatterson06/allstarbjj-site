## A 45-Year-Old Dad Asked Me If He'd Get Hurt

A guy from Millburn walked into All Star two weeks ago, stood on the mat edge, and said, "I just don't want to get crushed by a 25-year-old." Fair. He's built like a former high school lineman, hasn't rolled a day in his life, and assumed jiu-jitsu meant getting smashed until he tapped or broke something.

I told him the same thing I tell everyone: the entire art is built so a smaller, weaker, or older person can survive on the bottom without brute strength. That's not a sales pitch. It's the actual engineering of the sport. Let me show you with one concept — the frame.

## Bone, Not Muscle

When someone's on top of you in jiu-jitsu — mount, side control, doesn't matter — the instinct is to push them off with your arms. That's a losing fight. Their whole body weight against your bicep and tricep, they win every time, especially if they outweigh you by 40 pounds.

A frame is different. You angle your forearm or your shin across their hip, their throat line, their chest — and you brace it like a crossbar. You're not muscling them backward. You're creating a rigid structure between your skeleton and theirs so their weight has nowhere to go. Try pushing a heavy door open when someone's jammed a steel bar diagonally across the frame. Doesn't matter how strong you are. The geometry wins.

That's the whole secret to escapes. Elbow frame across the collarbone to stop someone crushing your chest in side control. Shin frame across the hips to stop them from settling their weight in when they're in your guard. You're not out-muscling a bigger opponent. You're using your bones like rebar and letting your structure do the work your muscles can't.

I walked that Millburn dad through this in his first fifteen minutes on the mat. By the end of the hour he was framing off one of our blue belts — half his age, in way better shape — and holding his own. Not because he got strong in fifteen minutes. Because the concept works regardless of strength.

## Position Before Submission

Here's the part that surprises people who've only seen jiu-jitsu on YouTube highlight reels — all armbars and chokes, nobody talks about how you got there.

Real coaching, the kind I got at Renzo Gracie's academy and the kind we run here in Union, teaches position before submission. Every single time. You do not go hunting for a finish until you own the position. Mount. Back control. Knee-on-belly. You secure that first, you make it boring and stable, and only then do you start working toward a tap.

This matters for a beginner more than anyone. It means when you're new and you get put in a bad spot — say, someone's got your back, hooks in, seatbelt grip — we're not throwing you to the wolves. We're teaching you the escape sequence, we're drilling the frame, we're building the survival instinct before we ever ask you to attack anything. You feel safe in bad positions because you understand exactly what's supposed to happen next. There's a checklist in your head, not panic.

That's the difference between a gym that just rolls people together and calls it class, versus actual coaching. I fought professionally — six years, IFL, Bellator, won the UWC light heavyweight title — and I promise you, position-first is not a beginner shortcut. It's how everyone above blue belt actually thinks. We just start teaching it on day one instead of hiding it.
